1     Introduction
1.1    Background
Knowing where a satellite is, and where it will be, is the basis of almost every space operation: pointing
antennas, planning manoeuvres, avoiding collisions and combining payload data with location. This task
is called orbit determination. In practice the satellite state cannot be observed directly. Instead, ground
stations measure quantities that depend on the state, such as the distance to the satellite (range) and
how fast that distance is changing (range-rate, obtained from the Doppler shift). These measurements
are noisy, and they are available only while the satellite is above the station’s horizon. A state estimator
is therefore needed to combine an imperfect dynamics model with noisy, intermittent measurements.

1.2    Objective
The aim of this mini project is to implement a state estimation filter for a specific application and to
demonstrate its effectiveness, as required by the assignment. The chosen combination is:
    • Filter: Extended Kalman Filter (EKF);
    • Application: satellite orbit determination from ground-station range and range-rate measurements.
The specific goals are to
    1. derive the dynamics and measurement models and their Jacobians;
    2. implement the EKF in MATLAB;
    3. evaluate it in simulation, using plots and statistical consistency tests (NEES and NIS, explained in
       Section 1.4);
    4. explore its capabilities and limitations by changing the scenario and the filter parameters.

1.3    Why an Extended Kalman Filter?
The standard Kalman filter is optimal for linear systems with Gaussian noise. Both parts of this problem
are nonlinear:
    • the gravitational acceleration −µ r/∥r∥3 is nonlinear in the position;
    • the range ρ = ∥r − rs ∥ and range-rate ρ̇ are nonlinear in the state.
As presented in the lectures (Gaussian Filters III), the EKF handles such systems by linearising the
state-transition and measurement models about the current estimate with a first-order Taylor expansion,
and then applying the Kalman filter equations. It is computationally cheap and it is the most widely
used nonlinear estimator, which makes it well suited to a six-dimensional state that is propagated for
many hours. A particle filter could also represent nonlinear problems, but it needs a very large number
of particles in six dimensions when the measurements are as accurate as here, so it is far more expensive
for little benefit. The orbit problem is also only mildly nonlinear over the time spans of interest, which is
the situation where the EKF works well.

1.4    How the EKF is checked: NEES and NIS
A filter should be accurate, but it should also be honest about how accurate it is. The EKF produces
a covariance matrix P that states how uncertain it believes its own estimate to be. To check that this
claim is true, two standard consistency tests are used throughout the results (Section 4):
    • NEES (Normalised Estimation Error Squared) compares the actual error of the estimate with the
      uncertainty the filter claims. It needs the true state, so it can be used in simulation only. For a
      consistent filter its average is about 6 (the number of states). Values much larger than this mean
      the filter is overconfident; values much smaller mean it is too cautious.



                                                     3
  • NIS (Normalised Innovation Squared) compares each new measurement with the prediction the
    filter made before seeing it, scaled by the predicted uncertainty. It needs no true state, so it can also
    be used with real data. For a consistent filter its average is about 2 (one range and one range-rate
    value per measurement).
Together with the ±3σ error bounds and the normalised innovations, these tests show whether the EKF
is correctly tuned. Their exact definitions are given in Section 2.7.

1.5    Report outline
Section 2 gives the theory: the models, the Jacobian derivations and the filter equations. Section 3
describes the simulation and the MATLAB implementation. Section 4 presents the results, and Section 5

2.6   The Extended Kalman Filter
Following the lecture derivation (Lecture 5, “From KF to EKF”), the EKF is the Kalman filter with the
linear models replaced by their Jacobians. The state-transition function is linearised about the previous
estimate and the measurement function about the predicted estimate. The prediction and correction
equations below are the lecture’s EKF algorithm, with the orbit dynamics in place of g and no control
input ut (the orbit is driven by gravity only). The symbols differ from the lecture in some places, so
Table 2 gives the correspondence. In particular, the lecture calls the process-noise covariance Rt and the
measurement-noise covariance Qt , while this report uses the opposite, more common textbook convention

The truth is produced by a different integrator (ode45) than the one used inside the filter (RK4), and it

while London and Moscow are at high northern latitudes, which a nearly polar orbit passes more often.

     3.3      Key code
     The measurement function and its Jacobian follow Equation (12) directly:
 1   d = x(1:3) - rs;                  % station-to-satellite vector
 2   dv = x(4:6) - vs;                 % relative velocity
 3   rho = norm(d);
 4   u   = d / rho;                    % line-of-sight unit vector
 5   rho_dot = u’ * dv;
 6
 7   h = [rho; rho_dot];
 8
 9   H = [u’,                          zeros(1, 3);
10        (dv - rho_dot * u)’ / rho,   u’];


     The prediction step propagates the state together with the state-transition matrix and then grows the
     covariance:
 1   [x, Phi] = propagate_state(x, dt, cfg, cfg.filter_uses_J2);
 2
 3   Q = process_noise(dt, cfg.q_accel);
 4   P = Phi * P * Phi’ + Q;
 5   P = (P + P’) / 2;                 % keep P symmetric


     The correction step, with the Joseph-form covariance update:
 1   [rs, vs] = station_eci(station, t, cfg);
 2   [h, H]   = range_model(x, rs, vs);
 3
 4   y = z - h;                        % innovation
 5   S = H * P * H’ + R;               % innovation covariance
 6   K = P * H’ / S;                   % Kalman gain
 7
 8   x = x + K * y;
 9
10   I_KH = eye(6) - K * H;
11   P = I_KH * P * I_KH’ + K * R * K’;% Joseph form


     The main loop predicts at every step and then corrects once for every station that currently sees the
     satellite:

                                                               10
 1   for k = 1:K
 2       if k > 1
 3           [x, P] = ekf_predict(x, P, truth.t(k) - truth.t(k-1), cfg);
 4       end
 5       for s = 1:ns
 6           if ~meas.visible(s, k), continue, end
 7           [x, P, y, S] = ekf_update(x, P, meas.z(:, s, k), ...
 8                                     stations(s), truth.t(k), R, cfg);
 9           ...
10       end
11   end


     3.4    Verification of the implementation
     Before running the full scenario, the main components were checked:
        • the analytical measurement Jacobian H was compared with central finite differences of h (maximum
          relative difference 5 × 10−8 );
        • the state-transition matrix Φ from the variational equations was compared with finite differences of
          the propagated state over 600 s (maximum relative difference 5 × 10−11 );
        • the RK4 propagator used in the filter was compared with the ode45 reference over 12 hours for
          the two-body + J2 model: the position difference was 0.23 m, much smaller than the filter errors
          reported below.
     The statistical consistency checks in Section 4.1 provide further evidence that the filter equations are
     implemented correctly. The whole 24-hour simulation and filter run takes about 0.6 s.

Estimation error. Figure 2 shows the size of the position and velocity error together with the filter’s
own one-sigma estimate (the root-sum-square of the diagonal of P), on a logarithmic scale. The grey
bands are the passes. The filter starts with a 549 m position error and a 2.7 m/s velocity error. The

slowly during the gaps, because the unmodelled drag and the remaining estimation error are propagated
forward without correction. The filter’s estimate of its own uncertainty follows the same pattern, mostly
slightly above the actual error.

Increasing the measurement noise by a factor of 10 increases the error by only about 50%. The accuracy

that each pass contains tens of epochs whose noise averages out, so that the accuracy is dominated by

4.3.6   Dynamics model: the role of J2

When the filter uses only two-body gravity, but the truth still contains J2 , the filter diverges completely
(RMS error 9.1 × 106 m), even though q was increased to 10−9 km2 /s3 to compensate. The reason is
that the J2 error accumulates as a large, systematic along-track drift of the order of 150 km over one
typical gap between passes (Section 2.2). That is far outside the range where the EKF linearisation is
valid (compare the initial-error study) and it is not white noise, so adding process noise cannot fix it. A
good dynamics model is more valuable than a large q.

5     Discussion
Effectiveness of the EKF. For the baseline scenario the EKF is very effective. It brings the position
error from 549 m (and a 2.7 m/s velocity error) down to a few metres after the very first pass, after the
first two hours it keeps the error below 12 m, and it does so while being able to see the satellite for only
10.5% of the time. Both the innovation statistics and the 3σ coverage show the filter is well calibrated. It
is also very cheap: a full 24-hour run takes 0.6 s on a laptop.

Why the accuracy is limited by the gaps and not the sensors. The results show a clear pattern:
the error is small right after each pass and grows between passes (Figure 2), and the accuracy barely
depends on the measurement noise (Table 9). A bigger improvement could therefore be gained from
better force modelling (for example estimating the drag coefficient as an extra state) than from better
ranging hardware.

Observability and the number of stations. Each epoch delivers only two numbers (a distance and
its rate of change) for a six-dimensional state, so the full state must be reconstructed from how the range
and Doppler curves change over a pass and from many passes. With a single station and a sparse pass
schedule (three passes in 24 hours, longest gap 10.5 hours) the information appears to be insufficient and
the filter diverges. With three to five stations the filter suffers from long gaps of 4.6 hours, and only with
six stations does the longest gap fall to 2.0 hours and the error stay at a few metres (Table 6). More
stations therefore help mainly by making the passes frequent enough to keep the error in the region where
the linearisation holds.

Limits of the EKF. The studies show three situations where the EKF breaks down or struggles: a
large initial error (50 km or more), an incomplete dynamics model (J2 left out), and too little data (one
station, or long gaps). In all of them the cause is the same: the filter linearises about an estimate that is
too far from the truth. Possible remedies include an iterated EKF (re-linearising the correction step), an
unscented Kalman filter, or using a batch least-squares fit of the first passes to obtain a better initial
state before switching to the EKF. A particle filter would also be robust against large initial errors but
would be much more expensive.

Tuning and conservatism. The process noise had a large effect. A value that is too small gives
overconfident (inconsistent) estimates and a value that is too large gives noisier ones. The baseline q is
slightly conservative, so the NEES is below its ideal value, but the real error is always within 3σ and the
NIS is almost exactly 2. For an operational system, a conservative covariance is usually preferred to an
overconfident one.

Limitations of this study.
    • The results come from simulation. The truth model is simpler than reality (constant drag, no
      third-body forces or solar pressure, no precession or nutation, no measurement biases or atmospheric
      delays), so the absolute error values should not be compared with real tracking systems.
    • The stations are treated as ideal range and Doppler sensors with the same noise at every location,
      and the station locations are only used as convenient, well-known positions on the globe.
    • The parameter studies use one seed per case (except the Monte Carlo test), so small differences,
      such as among the three-, four- and five-station cases, are not statistically proven.
    • The tracked satellite is in a single orbit; eccentric or higher-altitude orbits were not tested.

6    Conclusion
An Extended Kalman Filter for satellite orbit determination from ground-station range and range-rate
measurements was derived, implemented in MATLAB and evaluated in a 24-hour simulation with six
stations (Moratuwa, London, New York, Tokyo, Sydney and Moscow). The measurement Jacobian was
derived analytically, and the state-transition matrix was obtained by integrating the variational equations
of a two-body + J2 model. In the baseline scenario the filter reduced an initial error of 549 m and 2.7 m/s
to an RMS error of 4.0 m in position and 4.3 mm/s in velocity, with measurements available only 10.5%
of the time, and the NIS, normalised-innovation and 3σ tests showed that the filter is consistent. A Monte
Carlo test over 20 runs confirmed this (3.78 ± 0.33 m). A 3D animation of the satellite, the stations
and the tracking lines was also produced to illustrate the scenario. The parameter studies showed that
the accuracy depends strongly on the number and geometry of the stations (through the length of the
gaps between passes), on correct tuning of the process noise and on including J2 in the model, and only
weakly on the measurement noise. They also showed the main weakness of the EKF: it diverges when
the initial error is too large (about 50 km), when there is too little data (one station) or when the model
is wrong. Future work could estimate the drag coefficient as an additional state, compare the EKF with
an unscented Kalman filter and with batch least squares, and use real tracking data.

 1   function truth = simulate_truth(cfg)
 2   % Generate the true satellite trajectory (two-body + J2 + small drag) with a
 3   % high-accuracy ODE solver, independent of the filter’s own integrator.
 4   % The drag is unknown to the filter, so the filter must handle it with process noise.
 5
 6   t = 0:cfg.dt:cfg.T;
 7   x0 = kepler_to_state(cfg.orbit, cfg.mu);
 8
 9   drag = @(v) -cfg.drag_accel * v / norm(v); % opposes the velocity
10   f    = @(~, x) [x(4:6); orbit_accel(x(1:3), cfg, true) + drag(x(4:6))];
11   opts = odeset(’RelTol’, 1e-10, ’AbsTol’, 1e-12);
12   [~, X] = ode45(f, t, x0, opts);
13
14   truth.t = t;
15   truth.x = X’;                                % 6 x K
16   end

 1   function meas = simulate_measurements(cfg, truth, stations)
 2   % Noisy [range; range-rate] from each station, only while the satellite
 3   % is above the minimum elevation. Missing measurements are NaN.
 4
 5   K = numel(truth.t);
 6   ns = numel(stations);
 7   noise_std = [cfg.sigma_range; cfg.sigma_rrate];
 8
 9   meas.z       = nan(2, ns, K);
10   meas.visible = false(ns, K);
11
12   for k = 1:K
13       x = truth.x(:, k);
14       for s = 1:ns
15           [rs, vs, up] = station_eci(stations(s), truth.t(k), cfg);
16
17               d = x(1:3) - rs;
18               elevation = asind(dot(d, up) / norm(d));
19               if elevation < cfg.min_elev
20                   continue
21               end
22
23               h = range_model(x, rs, vs);
24               meas.z(:, s, k)   = h + noise_std .* randn(2, 1);
25               meas.visible(s, k) = true;
26         end
27   end

 1   function x = kepler_to_state(orbit, mu)
 2   % Convert Kepler elements to an inertial state x = [r; v] (km, km/s).
 3
 4   e = orbit.e;
 5   nu = deg2rad(orbit.nu);
 6   p = orbit.a * (1 - e^2);
 7
 8   % position and velocity in the orbital plane
 9   r_pf = p / (1 + e*cos(nu)) * [cos(nu); sin(nu); 0];
10   v_pf = sqrt(mu / p) * [-sin(nu); e + cos(nu); 0];
11
12   % rotate to the inertial frame

 1   function [rs, vs, up] = station_eci(st, t, cfg)
 2   % Ground station position, velocity and local "up" direction in the inertial
 3   % frame at time t. The station moves because the Earth rotates.
 4
 5   lat = deg2rad(st.lat);
 6   lon = deg2rad(st.lon);
 7
 8   % position in the Earth-fixed frame (WGS84 ellipsoid)
 9   N = cfg.Re / sqrt(1 - cfg.e2 * sin(lat)^2);
10   r_ecef = [(N + st.alt) * cos(lat) * cos(lon);
11             (N + st.alt) * cos(lat) * sin(lon);
12             (N * (1 - cfg.e2) + st.alt) * sin(lat)];
13   up_ecef = [cos(lat)*cos(lon); cos(lat)*sin(lon); sin(lat)];
14
15   % rotate into the inertial frame by the Earth rotation angle
16   theta = cfg.we * t;
17   Rz = [cos(theta) -sin(theta) 0; sin(theta) cos(theta) 0; 0 0 1];
18
19   rs = Rz * r_ecef;
20   up = Rz * up_ecef;
21   vs = cross([0; 0; cfg.we], rs);            % velocity from Earth rotation
22   end

 1   function a = orbit_accel(r, cfg, use_J2)
 2   % Gravity acceleration at position r (3x1, km): two-body plus optional J2.
 3
 4   rn = norm(r);
 5   a = -cfg.mu * r / rn^3;                     % two-body gravity
 6
 7   if use_J2
 8       k = -1.5 * cfg.J2 * cfg.mu * cfg.Re^2 / rn^5;
 9       z2 = (r(3) / rn)^2;
10       a_J2 = k * [r(1) * (1 - 5*z2);
11                   r(2) * (1 - 5*z2);
12                   r(3) * (3 - 5*z2)];
13       a = a + a_J2;
14   end
15   end

 1   function [x, Phi] = propagate_state(x, dt, cfg, use_J2)
 2   % Move the state x = [r; v] forward by dt using RK4.
 3   % Phi is the state transition matrix (the Jacobian F of the dynamics over dt).
 4
 5   n_steps = ceil(dt / cfg.max_step);
 6   h = dt / n_steps;
 7
 8   z = [x; reshape(eye(6), 36, 1)];            % state and Phi stacked together
 9   for i = 1:n_steps
10       k1 = derivative(z,            cfg, use_J2);
11       k2 = derivative(z + h/2 * k1, cfg, use_J2);
12       k3 = derivative(z + h/2 * k2, cfg, use_J2);
13       k4 = derivative(z + h   * k3, cfg, use_J2);
14       z = z + h/6 * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4);
15   end
16
17   x   = z(1:6);
18   Phi = reshape(z(7:end), 6, 6);
19   end
20
21   function dz = derivative(z, cfg, use_J2)
22   % Rate of change of the state and of Phi:    dPhi/dt = A * Phi
23   r   = z(1:3);
24   v   = z(4:6);
25   Phi = reshape(z(7:end), 6, 6);
26
27   a = orbit_accel(r, cfg, use_J2);
28   A = [zeros(3), eye(3);
29        accel_gradient(r, cfg, use_J2), zeros(3)];
30
31   dz = [v; a; reshape(A * Phi, 36, 1)];
32   end
33
34   function G = accel_gradient(r, cfg, use_J2)
35   % d(acceleration)/d(position), found by central finite differences.
36   d = 1e-3;                                   % small step (km)
37   G = zeros(3);
38   for i = 1:3
39       dr = zeros(3, 1);
40       dr(i) = d;
41       G(:, i) = (orbit_accel(r + dr, cfg, use_J2) - orbit_accel(r - dr, cfg, use_J2)) / (2*d);
42   end
43   end

 1   function [h, H] = range_model(x, rs, vs)
 2   % Measurement model: h = [range; range-rate] seen from a station.
 3   % H is the 2x6 Jacobian of h with respect to the state [r; v].
 4
 5   d = x(1:3) - rs;                              % station-to-satellite vector
 6   dv = x(4:6) - vs;                             % relative velocity
 7   rho = norm(d);
 8   u   = d / rho;                                 % line-of-sight unit vector
 9   rho_dot = u’ * dv;
10
11   h = [rho; rho_dot];
12
13   H = [u’,                             zeros(1, 3);
14        (dv - rho_dot * u)’ / rho,      u’];
15   end

     A.5     Extended Kalman Filter
 1   function [x, P] = ekf_predict(x, P, dt, cfg)
 2   % EKF prediction step: propagate the state and grow the uncertainty.
 3
 4   [x, Phi] = propagate_state(x, dt, cfg, cfg.filter_uses_J2);
 5
 6   Q = process_noise(dt, cfg.q_accel);
 7   P = Phi * P * Phi’ + Q;
 8   P = (P + P’) / 2;                              % keep P symmetric
 9   end
10
11   function Q = process_noise(dt, q)
12   % Random acceleration noise of strength q, integrated over dt.
13   I = eye(3);
14   Q = q * [dt^3/3 * I, dt^2/2 * I;
15            dt^2/2 * I, dt     * I];
16   end

                                                   Listing 11: ekf_predict.m

 1   function [x, P, y, S] = ekf_update(x, P, z, station, t, R, cfg)
 2   % EKF correction step using one station’s [range; range-rate] measurement z.
 3   % Returns the innovation y and its covariance S for consistency checks.
 4
 5   [rs, vs] = station_eci(station, t, cfg);
 6   [h, H]   = range_model(x, rs, vs);
 7
 8   y = z - h;                                    % innovation
 9   S = H * P * H’ + R;                           % innovation covariance
10   K = P * H’ / S;                               % Kalman gain
11
12   x = x + K * y;
13
14   I_KH = eye(6) - K * H;
15   P = I_KH * P * I_KH’ + K * R * K’;            % Joseph form (numerically safe)
16   end

 1   function est = run_ekf(cfg, truth, meas, stations)
 2   % Run the EKF over the whole time line.
 3   % Each step: predict, then correct once per visible station.
 4
 5   K = numel(truth.t);
 6   ns = numel(stations);
 7
 8   R   = diag([cfg.sigma_range, cfg.sigma_rrate].^2);
 9   P   = diag([cfg.P0_pos * ones(1, 3), cfg.P0_vel * ones(1, 3)].^2);
10
11   % start from a wrong initial guess drawn from the initial uncertainty
12   x = truth.x(:, 1) + sqrt(diag(P)) .* randn(6, 1);
13
14   est.x           = zeros(6, K);
15   est.sigma       = zeros(6, K);                  % filter’s own 1-sigma values
16   est.nees        = zeros(1, K);                  % state error consistency
17   est.innov       = nan(2, ns, K);
18   est.innov_sigma = nan(2, ns, K);
19   est.nis         = nan(ns, K);                   % innovation consistency
20
21   for k = 1:K
22       if k > 1
23           [x, P] = ekf_predict(x, P, truth.t(k) - truth.t(k-1), cfg);
24       end
25
26         for s = 1:ns
27             if ~meas.visible(s, k)
28                 continue
29             end
30             [x, P, y, S] = ekf_update(x, P, meas.z(:, s, k), stations(s), truth.t(k), R, cfg);
31
32               est.innov(:, s, k)       = y;
33               est.innov_sigma(:, s, k) = sqrt(diag(S));
34               est.nis(s, k)            = y’ / S * y;
35         end
36
37         e = x - truth.x(:, k);
38         est.x(:, k)     = x;
39         est.sigma(:, k) = sqrt(diag(P));
40         est.nees(k)     = e’ / P * e;
41   end
42   end
